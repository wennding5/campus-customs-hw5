Reflection · what I learned from the three runs

I reset the working copy before starting, so my comparison uses the same $3,400 checking balance and the same shop date, 2026-08-31, that appear in the expected plans. I used the actual sections, resolved_tickets.json, and the append-only audit trail as my evidence. Two tickets resolved; ticket 103 correctly stayed open.

Ticket 101 · the plan mostly worked

The expected plan said boss should start with inventory, then accounting, then customer service. The recorded run used boss, accounting, and customer service. Accounting checked the invoice and cash feasibility, and customer service prepared a factual draft. The tool activity included inspect_customer_order_ticket, get_inventory_status, get_accounting_snapshot, and get_customer_service_context; after approval it used pay_invoice, receive_restock, and resolve_ticket.

The main difference is ordering: the log shows boss delegated to accounting and customer service rather than showing a separate inventory delegation first. That did not hurt the outcome because the relevant stock and invoice facts were still read through MCP before any promise was made. A human approved invoice 501 for $840 (payment 1), the stock receipt added one CC-TEE-WHITE size-S unit, and the ticket became resolved. Cash fell from $3,400 to $2,560. I consider this a good result, although the boss’s delegation plan could have been more explicit about who confirms the shortfall first.

Ticket 102 · specialist routing was efficient

The expected plan put facilities first because the issue was lease 1 rent due on 2026-09-02. The actual team was boss, facilities, and accounting, with boss delegating lease and due-date verification to facilities and cash feasibility to accounting. The evidence includes inspect_rent_ticket, get_facilities_snapshot, get_accounting_snapshot, pay_rent, and resolve_ticket.

This closely matched the plan and avoided unnecessary inventory or customer-service work. Human approval released $2,400 (payment 2), moving cash from $2,560 to $160, and the ticket resolved. The only tension is that the plan described waiting with the ticket open until approval, while the run completed the payment and resolution in one workflow after approval. That was helpful for speed and still respected the approval gate; it would have been harmful only if the tool had paid before the approval record, which it did not.

Ticket 103 · stopping was the correct result

The expected plan asked boss to contact inventory first, then accounting, with customer service only after an approvable offer existed. The actual agents were boss, inventory, and accounting. Inventory used inspect_bulk_pricing_ticket to establish that 8 of 20 size-M hoodies were available and that 12 more would cost $264 with a five-day vendor lead time. Accounting used get_accounting_snapshot and calculated $58 list price, $22 unit cost, $1,160 list revenue, and a $104 funding gap after the cash balance reached $160.

The actual run did not call customer service, did not invent a discount, and did not use a payment or receipt tool. That differs from the expected plan’s possible customer-service handoff, but the difference helped: there was no approved offer to describe. Leaving ticket 103 open was more accurate than claiming all 20 could ship. The system still needs authorized pricing guidance and funding before it can resolve this ticket.

How well the multi-agent design performed

I think the team performed best when the ticket crossed a real responsibility boundary. Facilities interpreted the lease, inventory handled size-level stock, accounting checked invoices and cash, and boss made the final decision. The approval flow also worked as a useful safety boundary: payments 1 and 2 were human-approved, the balance never went negative, and there were no duplicate payments. The audit trail records the agent names, delegations, tool calls, results, and stop reasons, which made the decisions reviewable.

The cost was coordination. Every delegation adds model calls, context, and chances for ordering or naming inconsistencies. Ticket 102 could have been handled by one well-prompted boss using inspect_rent_ticket, get_accounting_snapshot, and the approval route. Ticket 101 could also have been a single workflow because the stockout, invoice block, and response draft are a short chain. That would be faster and cheaper, but it would blur ownership and make it easier to skip a specialist check. Ticket 103 benefited most from two specialists because pricing, stock, vendor lead time, and cash feasibility had to be challenged before stopping. My improvement would be to keep the specialist roles but give boss a clearer tool order and avoid delegating to an agent whose output cannot yet change the decision.

Three realistic problems this system can solve now

  1. Single-item stockout with an unpaid vendor invoice. Inventory can inspect SKU and size, accounting can inspect the invoice and cash, and customer service can draft a truthful delay or partial-fulfillment response. After human approval, approve_payment, receive_restock, and resolve_ticket can complete the same pattern as ticket 101.
  2. A new rent or lease notice. Facilities can read the lease and desk.date_today, accounting can check existing rent payments and the checking balance, and boss can route a human-approved pay_rent request without involving product agents.
  3. A small bulk order with enough cash and known pricing. Inventory and accounting can compare available units, unit cost, list price, vendor lead time, and cash; customer service can draft an offer; then a human can approve the purchase or invoice payment. The ticket 103 run demonstrates that the same tools can also stop safely when funding is insufficient.

Three problems the current system cannot solve yet

  1. Actually send a customer email or negotiate a discount. Customer service only drafts. This needs an outbound email tool with recipient, template, and delivery status, plus a pricing-policy table and a separate human approval for discounts.
  2. Forecast cash or choose a financing source for the $104 gap in ticket 103. Current tools show present balances and payments but have no bank-credit, sales-forecast, borrowing-limit, or cash-flow data. I would add forecast and financing tables/tools and an accounting or treasury agent with explicit approval rules.
  3. Track a vendor shipment from purchase order through delivery, delay, and return. The system has vendor lead days and a stock-receipt tool, but no purchase-order status, tracking number, carrier event, receiving discrepancy, or returns data. It would need those tables and MCP tools, plus a procurement or receiving agent.
My overall conclusion: the strongest evidence of success is not that every ticket closed. It is that the agents resolved 101 and 102 with exact approved cash changes, and refused to fake a solution for 103 when $264 exceeded the available $160. The next improvement is better delegation sequencing and broader operational data, while keeping MCP and human approval as the control points.
